The Late Cretaceous

From 100 to 66 million years ago, the Late Cretaceous period marked the final chapter of the Mesozoic Era. The continents were splitting apart, sea levels ran high, and vast inland seas divided what would one day become North America. Warm temperatures stretched pole to pole, and flowering plants bloomed for the first time across a world still ruled by reptiles.

Reign of the Titans

Tyrannosaurus rex stalked the floodplains of Laramidia while Triceratops grazed in herds along the forest edges. In the skies, Quetzalcoatlus — with a wingspan wider than a fighter jet — soared on thermal currents above shallow inland seas teeming with mosasaurs and giant ammonites. The Cretaceous was not short on monsters.

A World in Flux

Beneath the spectacle of predator and prey, the planet was already under stress. The Deccan Traps — a vast volcanic province in what is now India — had been erupting for thousands of years, pumping sulfur and carbon dioxide into the atmosphere. Climate swings grew erratic. Ecosystems that had been stable for millions of years began to fray at the edges long before the asteroid arrived.

The Boundary

Sixty-six million years ago, a bolide roughly 10 kilometers across struck the shallow sea at Chicxulub on the Yucatán Peninsula. The impact released energy equivalent to billions of nuclear weapons, triggering wildfires, tsunamis, and a years-long impact winter that shut down photosynthesis across the globe. The Cretaceous ended not with a slow fade but with a hard stop — and the world that followed would belong to an entirely different cast of characters.
